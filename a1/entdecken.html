1der · die · das (how German assigns gender)

Why does German have three genders, and how can you guess them? First the story, then some surprising facts, then the patterns that help you.

1 · Die Geschichte (the story)

Woher kommen drei Genera? (where the three genders came from)

  1. Early Proto-Indo-European: two classesProbably animate and inanimate, not male and female. Hittite, the oldest recorded Indo-European language, kept only this two-way split.
  2. Later: the feminine appearsThe animate class split in two. A widely held theory says the new feminine grew out of a suffix that formed abstract and collective nouns. This may be why abstract nouns still lean feminine (die Freiheit).
  3. Old High German (about 750–1050)German inherited all three genders. Endings on the noun wore away, so the article took over the job of showing gender and case.
  4. English and Dutch: cousins that dropped a genderEnglish has only one word for “the”. Old English once had three genders like German, but lost them by the Middle English period. Dutch, a close cousin of German, merged masculine and feminine into one group. So it has two words for “the”: de (de man, de vrouw) and het (het kind). German kept all three: der, die, das.
  5. 1880: Mark Twain's jokeThe American writer Mark Twain learned German and wrote a funny essay about it, “The Awful German Language”. He joked that in German “a young lady has no sex, while a turnip has”. He meant: das Mädchen (girl) is neuter, but die Rübe (turnip) is feminine. The neuter Mädchen is purely grammatical: the ending -chen makes every word neuter. Read about the essay · full text (Appendix D, free)
  6. TodayNew loanwords get a gender within a few years. Regions still differ (das / die Cola), and some words accept two genders (das / der Virus).

2 · Überraschende Fakten (surprising facts)

Gut zu wissen (good to know)

The ending wins over the meaning.
das Mädchen and das Fräulein are neuter although they mean girls and women. die Wache (guard) is feminine even for a man.
Gender is not about sex.
“Car” is das Auto in German, la voiture in French and el coche in Spanish. It is a noun class, a filing system.
It helps listeners.
Hearing der or die rules out many nouns before the noun comes.
Children learn the patterns early.
Research on children learning German suggests they pick up ending and sound patterns early, long before they know any rules.

3 · Die Muster (the patterns)

Wie oft? (how common is each?)

Take 100 German nouns from the Duden dictionary: about 46 are die, 34 are der and 20 are das.

Was sind Duden, CELEX und HaGenLex? (what are they?)

Duden: the standard German dictionary. Konrad Duden published the first edition in 1880 with about 27,000 words. Today the Duden has about 151,000 headwords of all kinds, not only nouns.

CELEX: a research database of words in English, Dutch and German, kept by the Max Planck Institute for Psycholinguistics in the Netherlands. Its German part has about 50,700 base words (lemmas) and 359,600 word forms.

HaGenLex: a computer lexicon of German made at the FernUniversität in Hagen (a city in Germany). It is used in text-search and question-answering programs. The name means Hagen German Lexicon.

A lemma is the dictionary form of a word, for example Haus. The forms Häuser and Hauses count as word forms.

Merke: if you know nothing about a noun, die is the best guess (right 46 times in 100, more than der or das). You can do much better with the endings and meaning groups below.

Plural: always die
der Tisch → die Tische · das Kind → die Kinder · die Frau → die Frauen
Learn the gender only in the singular.

💡 Trick: for roughly 8 in 10 nouns, the ending, the meaning or the plural tells you the gender (a rough estimate). So you only need to memorise a small list of real exceptions.

    If no ending decides, the meaning often does. An ending still wins: das Mädchen is neuter because of -chen.

    Gleiches Wort, anderer Artikel (same word, different gender)

    The article changes the meaning. Learn these as pairs.

    Warum? (why?)These are really different words that look and sound the same. Linguists call them Homonyme. They have different origins, so each word kept its own article and often its own plural.
    • die Band (music group) is borrowed from English. das Band (ribbon) and der Band (book volume) belong to binden (to tie, to bind).
    • der See / die See: in older German one word meant both lake and sea. Later the two meanings split, and the article helps keep them apart.
    So a new article means a new word. Learn article, meaning and plural together.

    Regional (both can be right)

    Zusammengesetzte und fremde Wörter (compounds and loanwords)

    Compound: the last part decides.
    die Donaudampfschifffahrtsgesellschaft is feminine because of -schaft.
    Only the last part counts, however long the word is.

    New and borrowed words get a gender by these strategies, roughly in this order (according to the Duden, as summarised by the University of Michigan):

    1. Keep the original gender: das Museum, der Alligator
    2. Copy a German equivalent: der Computer (der Rechner) · die Mail (die Post)
    3. Follow the ending: die Garage, die Pizza · das Management
    4. English one-syllable verbs → der: der Chat, der Drink, der Sound

    Und danach? Fälle (the article changes with the case)

    The gender stays fixed. The article changes with the job of the noun in the sentence. At A1 you need the nominative and the accusative.

    Warum? (why?)

    1. The article shows who does what. In English, word order tells you: “the dog bites the man” and “the man bites the dog” mean different things. German can move words around, so the article does that job:

    Der Hund beißt den Mann. = Den Mann beißt der Hund.
    Both mean: the dog bites the man. der = the one doing it, den = the one it happens to.

    2. Why only der changes. Neuter words have used the same form for subject and object since the oldest Indo-European languages. In German, the feminine forms also became the same over time. Only the masculine kept a separate object form: den.

    3. English still has a trace of this. English pronouns change in the same way: he → him, I → me, who → whom (“He sees him”). German does it with the article too.

    casem derf dien daspl. die
    Nom. subjectderdiedasdie
    Acc. objectdendiedasdie
    Dat. indirectdemderdemden (+n)
    Gen. possessiondes (+s)derdes (+s)der

    Dative and genitive come later (A2 and above). Only the masculine changes in the accusative: der → den.

    💡 Plural trick: -en plurals are mostly feminine nouns (die Frau → die Frauen). -er plurals are never feminine (das Kind → die Kinder).

    2Deutsch kurios (fun facts about German)

    Where German is spoken, why it looks the way it does, and why you already know more of it than you think.

    1 · Die Sprache (the language)

    Wo spricht man Deutsch? (where is German spoken?)

    Six countries.
    German is an official language in: Deutschland, Österreich, die Schweiz, Liechtenstein, Luxemburg, Belgien. Austria and Switzerland have their own standard words: der Jänner (Austria) = der Januar.
    About 95–100 million native speakers.
    That is the most native speakers of any language in the EU. 95 = fünfundneunzig (FUENF-oont-NOYN-tsikh).
    Dialects can be very different.
    Swiss German (Schwiizerdütsch) is so different that Swiss speakers on German TV sometimes get subtitles. In Bavaria and Austria people say Grüß Gott (hello) and Servus (hi / bye).
    English is a cousin.
    English and German are both West Germanic languages. That is why so many basic words look alike: Haus / house, Wasser / water, Hand / hand.

    2 · Die Schrift (the writing)

    Groß, ß und Umlaut (capitals, ß and umlauts)

    Every noun starts with a capital.
    German is the only major language that does this: der Tisch, die Liebe, das Essen. English writers often did it too in the 1700s.
    Tip: a capital letter in the middle of a sentence = a noun = it has der, die or das.
    ß is two letters in one.
    The Eszett (“s-z”) began as a joined long ſ and z (or s) in old handwriting. A capital ẞ has been official only since 2017. Switzerland and Liechtenstein do not use ß at all: they write ss (Strasse).
    ß or ss? Listen to the vowel.
    Long vowel → ß: die Straße, der Fuß.
    Short vowel → ss: der Fluss, dass.
    This rule came with the spelling reform of 1996 (before: daß).
    The two dots were once a small e.
    Old scribes wrote a tiny e above a, o, u. It shrank into two dots. So when there are no umlauts on the keyboard, write ae, oe, ue: Müller → Mueller.

    Zahlen rückwärts (numbers backwards)

    From 21 to 99, German says the ones first, then the tens: “seven-and-forty”.

    ZahlDeutschWort für Wort (word by word · say it)
    21einundzwanzigone-and-twenty

    83dreiundachtzigthree-and-eighty

    Old English did it too.
    The old nursery rhyme “Sing a Song of Sixpence” has “four and twenty blackbirds” (24). Dutch still counts like German: zevenenveertig (47).
    Written as one word.
    Numbers under a million are one long word: 365 = dreihundertfünfundsechzig.
    Telefon: zwo.
    On the phone Germans often say zwo instead of zwei (2), so it does not sound like drei (3).

    💡 Trick: when you hear a number, write the second digit first. Hear “sieben-und-vierzig” → leave a gap, write 7, then 4 in front: 47.

    5 · Die Verwandten (the relatives)

    Englisch ↔ Deutsch: Lautregeln (sound rules between English and German)

    About 1,500 years ago, some consonants changed in southern German but not in English. Learn the pattern and you can guess many words.

    English→ GermanPaare (pairs)
    tz / ssten → zehn · water → Wasser · eat → essen
    pf / pfship → Schiff · apple → Apfel · pepper → Pfeffer
    kchmake → machen · book → Buch
    dtday → Tag · drink → trinken
    thdthree → drei · thank → danke · thing → Ding

    Most of these come from the “High German consonant shift” (about 500–800 AD); th → d is a separate, later change. Not every word follows them, but many do.

    6 · Die Wörter (the words)

    Lange, geliehene und falsche Wörter (long, borrowed and “fake English” words)

    63 letters, one word.
    das Rindfleischetikettierungsüberwachungsaufgabenübertragungsgesetz was a real law in Mecklenburg-Vorpommern. It was repealed in 2013.
    Word by word: Rindfleisch (beef) + Etikettierung (labelling) + Überwachung (monitoring) + Aufgaben (tasks) + Übertragung (transfer) + Gesetz (law) = “law on transferring the task of monitoring beef labelling”. It is das because the last part is das Gesetz.

    7 · Der Satz (the sentence)

    Satzbau und Höflichkeit (word order and politeness)

    The verb is always second.
    Heute lerne ich Deutsch.
    Today learn I German. → Today I learn German. Whatever comes first, the verb stays in position 2.
    Some verbs split in two.
    anrufen (to call): Ich rufe dich morgen an.
    I call you tomorrow up. → I'll call you tomorrow. The small part jumps to the end.
    After “dass” and “weil”, the verb goes last.
    Ich weiß, dass du Deutsch lernst.
    I know that you German learn. → I know that you are learning German.
    Three words for “you”.
    du (one friend), ihr (friends), Sie (polite, one or many). English once had this too: thou was the informal “you”. Polite Sie is really “they” with a capital S.

    8 · Die Geschichte (the history)

    Wie Hochdeutsch entstand (how standard German came about)

    1. About 1450: Gutenberg in MainzJohannes Gutenberg's printing press made books cheap. Printers wanted a German that readers in many regions could understand.
    2. 1522 and 1534: Luther's BibleMartin Luther translated the New Testament (1522) and then the whole Bible (1534) into German that ordinary people spoke. It was printed in huge numbers and helped shape one written German for all regions.
    3. 1880: the first DudenKonrad Duden's dictionary set common spelling rules (see topic 1).
    4. 1996: spelling reformNew rules for ß/ss and more: daß → dass. Small updates still come from the Rat für deutsche Rechtschreibung (Council for German Orthography), for example the capital ẞ in 2017.
